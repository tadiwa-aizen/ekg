elow is a practical, UCT‑compatible guide for your front matter, Chapter 1 (Intro), Chapter 2 (Lit Review), and Chapter 3 (Methodology), assuming a ~20–25k word CS MSc dissertation.

I’ll give you: purpose, rough word counts, and suggested subheadings for each.

Front matter
Purpose: Give all administrative and navigational info before the actual argument starts.

Include, in this order (no word count stress here):

Title page (per UCT template: title, name, degree, department, supervisors, date).

Declaration (UCT plagiarism/declaration text as required by Faculty of Science or School of IT guidelines).

Abstract (200–300 words, written last):

1–2 sentences: context and problem.

1–2 sentences: approach/methods at high level.

2–3 sentences: key results and what they show.

1 sentence: main contribution and implication.

Acknowledgements (optional, 0.5–1 page).

Table of contents.

List of figures and list of tables.

List of abbreviations and symbols (especially for ML/graph terms).
​

Chapter 1 – Introduction (about 10–15% of total, say 2,000–3,000 words)
Goal: Set up the problem, why it matters, what you’ll do, and how the thesis is structured.

Suggested structure:

1.1 Background and context

Introduce the broad area (e.g., event‑centric knowledge graphs / information extraction / evaluation).

Briefly describe real‑world or research context motivating this (e.g., need for robust evaluation frameworks).

1.2 Problem statement

Clearly state the core problem as 1–2 tight paragraphs: what is missing or broken in current practice/literature.

Make the “gap” explicit: “Although X, Y, and Z exist, there is no …”.

1.3 Research aim, objectives, and questions

One overall aim: a single sentence capturing what your thesis wants to achieve.

3–5 objectives, each starting with a strong verb (design, implement, compare, evaluate).

1–3 research questions, each answerable by your methods and results.

1.4 Scope and delimitations

Clarify what you do and do not cover: datasets, task types, languages, metrics, systems, etc.

Briefly justify key exclusions (e.g., “deep learning model design is out of scope; we focus on evaluation frameworks”).
​

1.5 Significance and contributions

Explain why solving this problem matters: to research, industry, or specific application domains.

List 2–4 concrete contributions, for example:

“A conceptual framework for evaluating event‑centric knowledge graphs …”

“An implementation and open‑source toolkit for …”

“An empirical comparison of metrics A, B, C on datasets D, E, F …”.

1.6 Thesis structure

One paragraph per chapter, 1–2 sentences each, explaining what’s in Chapters 2–6.

Keep this synced with any later re‑ordering.

Tips:

Write a rough version early, then refine once methods and results are clearer.

Keep technical detail light; that’s for Chapters 2–3.

Chapter 2 – Literature review (about 20–25%, say 4,000–5,000 words)
Goal: Show what is known, what is missing, and how your work fits and fills a gap.

Overall shape: short intro, structured body (by themes), and a synthesis that leads directly to your research questions.

Suggested structure:

2.1 Introduction to the literature review

1–2 paragraphs explaining what this chapter will do and how it is organised (by themes, methodology, or chronology).

2.2 Conceptual and theoretical foundations

Define core concepts and terminology used throughout: events, knowledge graphs, ontologies, evaluation metrics, etc.

Briefly outline any theoretical frameworks or taxonomies you will rely on later.

2.3 Prior work on [your main topic]
Break this into clear subsections, for example:

2.3.1 Construction of event‑centric knowledge graphs (data sources, extraction methods, representations).

2.3.2 Evaluation of knowledge graphs (intrinsic vs extrinsic, standard metrics, benchmarks).

2.3.3 Frameworks and toolkits related to evaluation (any existing evaluation frameworks or platforms).
Within each subsection:

Group related studies together (by approach, methodology, or use‑case).

Critically compare: who did what, with which methods, strengths/limitations, how results relate.

2.4 Methodological patterns in the literature

Summarise common research designs (e.g., experimental set‑ups, datasets, baselines, evaluation practices) that similar studies use.

Highlight methodological weaknesses (e.g., small datasets, inconsistent metrics, missing baselines) that motivate your own choices.

2.5 Identified gaps and research opportunities

Explicitly unpack where the literature falls short: missing frameworks, inconsistent evaluation, lack of standardised metrics, etc.

Link each gap to one or more of your research questions/objectives.

2.6 Chapter summary

1–3 paragraphs that:

Summarise the key themes.

Re‑state the main gaps.

Transition to methodology: “Given these gaps, the next chapter describes the research design used to develop and evaluate …”.

Tips:

Keep it critical (compare, contrast, evaluate), not just descriptive.

Use consistent citation style per UCT CS / library guide, and maintain a reference manager from day one.

Chapter 3 – Methodology (about 15–20%, say 3,000–4,000 words)
Goal: Explain how you designed and executed the study, and why those choices are appropriate, in enough detail for another researcher to reproduce or extend it.

Suggested structure (adapt to your specific design, e.g., framework design + experimental evaluation):

3.1 Research design and approach

State the overall design: experimental, design‑science, case study, or mixed.

Briefly describe whether your study is primarily qualitative, quantitative, or mixed, and why this is suitable.

Optionally mention research philosophy or paradigm if your supervisor cares about that language (e.g., positivist, pragmatist).

3.2 Data, corpora, and study context

Describe datasets or corpora (e.g., event datasets, text sources, existing KGs, benchmarks): origin, size, characteristics, preprocessing.

Explain why each dataset is appropriate for your research questions (coverage, difficulty, representativeness).

3.3 System design and implementation

High‑level architecture of your framework / system / pipeline (can reference a figure).

Key components and technologies (databases, libraries, graph stores, evaluation tools).

Design decisions that are critical to understanding the later results (e.g., how events are represented, how metrics are computed).

3.4 Experimental setup and procedures

Define baselines and comparison systems, if applicable, and justify selection.

Describe procedures step by step so they are replicable:

How you construct or ingest data.

How you run experiments (number of runs, random seeds, cross‑validation, etc.).

How you log and store results.

Specify any hyper‑parameters, environment details (hardware, OS, major library versions) that affect reproducibility.

3.5 Evaluation metrics and analysis methods

Clearly define all metrics (e.g., precision, recall, F1, graph‑specific metrics, task‑specific measures) with formulas where helpful.

Explain any composite scores or frameworks you propose.

Outline statistical tests or analysis procedures you’ll use (e.g., significance testing, effect sizes, error analysis).

3.6 Validity, reliability, and limitations

Discuss potential threats to internal, external, construct, and conclusion validity, where relevant for your design.

Explain how you mitigated or monitored them (e.g., using multiple datasets, robust baselines, ablations).

3.7 Ethical considerations

If using real‑world or potentially sensitive data, mention consent, anonymisation, and adherence to UCT’s ethics requirements.

Confirm that you follow academic integrity and data‑handling guidelines.

3.8 Chapter summary

Brief recap of design, data, implementation, and evaluation approach.

Short lead‑in to the results chapter: “The next chapter presents the results of applying this framework to …”.

Tips:

Err slightly on the side of too much detail for methods and environment; you can push code/configs to an appendix or Git repo, but the main text must be enough to follow.

Use diagrams or tables to summarise pipelines, datasets, or experiment grids where they would otherwise be wordy.
